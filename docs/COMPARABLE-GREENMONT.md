# Comparable: Greenmont Mutual Housing Corporation (Kettering, Ohio)

**Why this document exists.** Greenmont Village is the closest real-world operating comparable to the structure this model underwrites: a resident-owned housing cooperative in the Dayton metro, ~20 minutes from the subject property, that has run continuously since 1942. It is the single best answer to "has anyone actually done this, and does it last?" — and several of its operating facts should discipline our assumptions.

**Sources** (retrieved 2026-07-27; figures are as-published and carry the site's own "as of" dates):
- https://greenmontmutual.org/faq/
- https://greenmontmutual.org/residential-housing/
- https://greenmontmutual.org/about/
- https://livingnewdeal.org/sites/greenmont-village-kettering-oh/

Everything in §1–§3 is quoted or closely paraphrased from those pages. §4 is our inference and is labelled as such.

## 1. What it is

- One of **eight** communities built 1941–42 by the **Mutual Ownership Defense Housing Division, Federal Works Agency**, under Lawrence Westbrook's **Mutual Home Ownership Plan** (Lanham Act; CIO-backed). Became **Greenmont Mutual Housing Corporation** in December 1942.
- **500 units in 300 buildings on 137 acres.** Unit mix: 1-BR double (single storey), 2-BR double, 3-BR double, 2-BR single (two storey; 2-BR singles reserved for internal transfers).
- On-site: **Greenmont Elementary School** (Kettering School District), event hall, commercial/retail centre, community centre, parks and garden plots.
- A **private non-profit corporation** — explicitly **not** subsidised housing. Self-described as **middle-income**.
- Members hold **1 share, 1 vote**; the corporation owns the property and members hold occupancy agreements.

### The 1947 disposition — a direct precedent for our structure

> "Under the terms of the Mutual Plan, residents bought equal shares in a non-profit corporation … which **leased and later, with the shareholders' consent, purchased the entire community from the federal government**." (July 1947, Public Housing Authority reported Greenmont sold to the non-profit corporation.) "Once the sale was complete, the mutual ownership housing corporation **held the land title**."

And the fee formula they adopted on purchase:

> "They paid a monthly fee to cover **amortization of the mortgage, mortgage interest, depreciation, repairs, insurance, vacancy losses, operating expenses, taxes, and a reserve account**."

Dues stated "as of May 2022," rates subject to change.

**What dues cover:** "property taxes, trash, water, and operational/property maintenance assessments." The FAQ separately states "All utilities are the responsibility of the resident," which — read against the residential page — means **gas, electric and cable are resident-paid while water and trash sit inside the dues**. Worth confirming by phone before relying on it.

**Insurance split:** corporation insures **the original 1942 structure only**, restoring to original condition; it does not cover upgrades, additions or sheds. Every member must carry **≥$300,000 liability**, plus HO-4 (contents) or HO-6 (co-op/condo, for improvements and additions).

## 3. Equity, transfer, governance, admission

**This is the finding that matters most.**

- "Members **cannot buy or sell their units directly**"; they have "a significant voice in decision-making, and **a lifetime right to live in the housing**."
- Ownership is "**a perpetual lease**." A member "may **transfer or designate a beneficiary after ten (10) years** as a member, provided you have properly maintained your unit."
- **Subletting is not permitted.**
- One-year probationary period; after that, 45 days' notice to terminate.
- Annual unit inspections against published Property Maintenance Standards; monthly yard inspections. "Any unrecoverable damages done to the properties are **shared equally by all members**."

**Governance:** Board of Trustees of **six internal (resident) members and three external community members**, elected by the membership. Quarterly membership meetings (Zoom available); attendance not mandatory but required in order to vote. Nine committees: Resident Selection, Membership, Rules and Regulations, Building and Grounds, Community and Commercial Property, Finance, Activities, **Roof**, Annual Election. Governing documents are consolidated in "The Green Book" (Articles, Code of Regulations, Management Code, Committee Charters).

**Explicitly self-managed** — "Because we are self-managed, we have committees that work to improve the property" — supported by a **Manager of Property and Maintenance and supporting staff**.

**Admission criteria (as of 2022):** U.S. citizen; 21+; **FICO ≥ 630**; **debt-to-income ≤ 40%**; no prior felony convictions; criminal background check for every resident over 18; no bankruptcy discharged within one year; interview attended by applicant **and** spouse. Gross income **minimums**: $25,000 (1 person), $29,000 (2), $34,000 (3), $46,000 (4), +$3,500 per additional member. If an applicant turns down an offered unit, they must reapply from the start.

## 4. What this implies for our model (our inference, not Greenmont's claims)

**4.1 Member share capital is real, and now benchmarked.** $3,500/unit is a live local number. At 25 units that is **$87,500** — squarely inside the $75k–$125k range already tested, which moved investor IRR from 7.3% to 12.1% while lowering rent in both phases. This retires the question of whether member equity is a realistic ask in this market. It is standard practice twenty minutes away.

**4.2 Permanent affordability came from the no-resale rule, not from a land trust.** Greenmont holds its own land title — there is no CLT — yet it has stayed affordable for 84 years because **members cannot sell their units** and transfer runs through beneficiary designation rather than a market. This corrects a claim made earlier in this project's discussion: the *co-op corporate structure alone* can preserve affordability if the bylaws forbid direct sale. Our CLT ground lease is therefore **belt-and-braces, not the sole mechanism** — its distinct job is protecting the land if the co-op itself ever fails, converts, or is pressured to sell. Both layers are worth having, but the bylaws' resale prohibition is doing more work than we credited.

**4.3 Our management assumption is too aggressive.** Greenmont self-manages *500 units* and still employs a property/maintenance manager plus staff. Twenty-five units cannot spread that overhead. Model management as a contracted or shared service; do not underwrite Greenmont-style self-management savings at our door count. The corollary is an opportunity — see 4.7.

**4.4 We cannot underwrite by selection, and that is a real risk difference.** Greenmont screens for FICO ≥630, income minimums, DTI ≤40% and clean records. A conversion-in-place inherits its residents; screening them is neither available nor consistent with an anti-displacement mission. Our credit, collection and turnover risk is therefore **structurally higher than Greenmont's**, which argues that the model's 4% vacancy/bad-debt allowance is optimistic for the transition years and that reserves should sit at the deeper end. Flagged as a defaults question, not yet changed.

**4.5 The 1947 fee formula validates our rent build-up.** Amortisation + interest + depreciation + repairs + insurance + **vacancy losses** + operating expenses + taxes + **reserve account** is, line for line, the cost-recovery stack this engine computes — including the vacancy gross-up and the replacement reserve that earlier versions of this project were criticised for adding. That formula has been in continuous use since 1947.

**4.6 Phased acquisition has precedent.** Residents **leased first and purchased later** with shareholder consent. Our two-phase structure (seller note, then buyout refinance) is a variation on a pattern that has already worked in this state.

**4.7 Concrete opportunities to pursue.**
- **Shared administration / mentorship.** Greenmont has 84 years of co-op-specific back-office capacity in this metro and would be a credible reference for a Phase-2 lender who has never underwritten a co-op. Office: 20 Rembrandt Boulevard, Kettering OH 45420 — 937.252.9941 — office@greenmontmutual.org.
- **Outside directors.** Their 6-internal/3-external board is worth copying: outside trustees add capacity and lender comfort without diluting resident control.
- **Non-residential income.** Greenmont rents its event hall to the public and leases commercial space. Our 3.58 acres and the standalone house may support an analogous non-rent revenue line.
- **Insurance structure.** Corporation covers original structure only; members carry HO-6 plus $300k liability. That materially shrinks the co-op's insurance line and is worth pricing against our $22,500 assumption.
- **A dedicated Roof Committee.** Capital planning as a standing governance function, not an afterthought — cheap to copy, and it pairs with the replacement-reserve line.

**4.8 They pay property taxes.** An 84-year-old non-profit housing cooperative in the same metro carries property taxes inside its dues. This is real-world confirmation of the conclusion in ANALYSIS.md A.15: the ORC 5709.12 charitable-exemption path is a long shot, and the CRA / restricted-rent-valuation route is the realistic one.

**4.9 Comparing dues to our rent needs care.** $509–$519 (May 2022) is not directly comparable to our $700 policy rent: different inclusions (their residents pay gas/electric/cable), a four-year gap, unit-size mix, and — decisively — **Greenmont carries no acquisition debt of the kind this deal creates**. Their mortgage was retired long ago. The number is useful as a floor-of-the-possible and as evidence of what a mature co-op costs to run, not as a target for year one.
